\section{What the references can and cannot tell apart}\label{sec:results}
\paragraph{Population versus context-only.} Figure~\ref{fig:gap} shows the held-out gaps against context length and
Table~\ref{tab:maps} summarises them at horizon $h=1$; each map has its own grid of context lengths, given in the table. With a known noise scale the population gap is
\OneAGapMin--\OneAGapMax nats per forecast in the cells where it is separable, all at very short contexts; summed over all
context lengths these gaps cannot exceed the \KLoneA-nat budget (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The first study (Stage~0, a different comparator and rule)
found \SzeroAllPairs of \SzeroCells regimes in which all three strategy pairs were separable. \sHalf, pre-registered with
fresh seeds, confirmed a population-specific advantage at one-step but not at five-step horizons and returned the decision
REVISE. With observation noise (F3) the largest $h=1$ gap is \FthreeGapQzero, \FthreeGapQone, \FthreeGapQtwo and
\FthreeGapQthree nats at $q=0,\QOne,\QTwo,\QThree$: the same budget spread over more forecasts. No cell is separable at the
two larger noise levels or at $h=5$, and the pre-registered \sSix study had already returned STOP for long contexts. At
moderate noise the F3 map is underpowered: its realised-target estimator has standard errors several times those of the 1A
map, and \FthreeBorderline of its $h=1$ cells (\FthreeBorderlineQone at $q=\QOne$) are borderline although their point
estimates already reach $\delta$. The noise-free F3 row agrees with the 1A map on the three cells compared ($r=1$, $n\le 5$),
but the rows differ elsewhere for two reasons visible in the table: the grids differ (only F3 has $n$ between six and nine,
which is why its window at \rsmall ends later), and at $r=4$ the F3 cells are borderline or not separable where the more
precise 1A map is separable for the shortest contexts.

When the noise scale is unknown (F1) the gaps are larger, \FoneGapMin--\FoneGapMax nats, and separable up to $n=\FoneMaxN$ at
every $r$. Most of this is knowledge of the noise level: \KLFoneSigma of the \KLFone-nat F1 budget is the noise-scale part
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}), and its size is set by how wide we made the context-only noise prior. In the bivariate family (F2)
the population gap is up to \FtwoGapMax nats at $n=10$ and \FtwoGapMin--\FtwoGapLongMax nats at the longest mapped context,
$n=\FtwoNmax$, where it is still separable at every $r$, so the end of the F2 window is not mapped; the shortest grid point is
\textsc{mc-limited}: its gaps are large but the context-only reference cannot be estimated there to our Monte Carlo criterion.
The F2 budget, \KLFtwo nats, falls to \KLFtwoNM when the context-only reference is given the population's noise prior.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the margin.} Table~\ref{tab:delta} repeats the held-out comparison at half and twice the
margin. The window moves with $\delta$: with a known noise scale it extends to $n=\OneAMaxNHalfDelta$ at the smaller margin
and shrinks to the two shortest contexts at the larger one. At the smaller margin the known- and unknown-scale windows end at
the same grid point (the next grid point is twice as long in both maps); the bivariate window reaches the end of its grid at
every margin; and the two noisiest settings separate at no margin in this range.

\paragraph{Library versus continuous prior.} In the scalar families, small libraries are separable from continuous-prior Bayes
on held-out systems and large ones are not; in the bivariate family even the largest mapped libraries are. In \sHalf, a
library of two systems was distinguishable from the continuous prior on held-out systems in \LibSfiveDist of \LibSfiveCells
confirmatory cells. In the 1A map a single-system library is separable in \LibOneAKone of \LibOneACells held-out cells, and
every cell with \LibBigKOneA or more systems is not separable, as the nearest-system limit predicts (it falls below $\delta$
between four and eight systems; Section~\ref{sec:theory}). In the F1 map every one- and two-system cell is separable and
\LibFoneKfour of \LibFoneCells four-system cells are; with \KSixteen or \KThirtyTwo systems every cell is
\textsc{partial}, and with the largest library every cell is not separable. The F1 limit is above $\delta$ at
$K=\KSixteen$ (\NaUnknownSixteen nats) and just below it at $K=\KThirtyTwo$, so libraries of \KSixteen systems should become
separable at contexts longer than the map's longest ($n=\FoneLibNmax$), before which the library posterior has not yet
concentrated. In F3 no held-out cell is separable, but for two-system libraries the mean gap
reaches \FthreeLibKtwo nats and the cells are \textsc{partial}. Because a trained model sees one library, Table~\ref{tab:library}
and Figure~\ref{fig:library} give the share of individual (cell, library) pairs that are separable: it falls steeply with
library size in every scalar family and stays near one in the bivariate family, in the order the long-context limits predict.

On in-library systems the first map found library and continuous posteriors equivalent within $\delta$ in all
\SzeroElibEquiv cells. In the bivariate family the picture changes: on held-out systems a library of \KSixteen systems is
separable in \FtwoLibSixteen of \FtwoLibCells cells and a library of \FtwoKmax in \FtwoLibSixtyFour of \FtwoLibCells, in line
with a nearest-system limit of \NaTwoSixtyFour nats at $K=\FtwoKmax$: a library of this size covers a seven-parameter space
far more sparsely than an interval of scalar coefficients. In short, a library reference of a few scalar systems is separable
from population Bayes by score on held-out systems; with a known noise scale one of \LibBigKOneA or more is not anywhere in
our map; with an unknown noise scale the separable share falls steeply between eight and \KSixteen systems over the mapped
contexts; and a bivariate library of \FtwoKmax systems is still separable in most cells.
