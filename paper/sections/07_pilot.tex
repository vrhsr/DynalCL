\section{A pre-registered neural pilot that could not be resolved}\label{sec:pilot}
We pre-registered a neural study in the known-scale family (questions: population-like versus context-only behaviour in the
identifiable cells; library size at which memorisation gives way to generalisation), froze its code, configuration,
decision rules and seed table, and ran a pilot with dedicated seeds. The frozen rules chose the training budget as the
smallest grid point at which every control passed and the validation log loss at the shortest contexts stopped improving
by more than \ConvTol nats, then capped the number of models per arm by a compute budget, with a minimum of \MinModels
models per arm fixed before the pilot from the bootstrap-calibrated operating characteristics. The budget was raised from
\CapBudgetHoursOriginal to \CapBudgetHours GPU-hours by a pre-pilot amendment, made after seeing the timing benchmark and
before any pilot data, and frozen with the rest.

The pilot chose \PilotB training steps: at the previous grid point the loss still improved by \PilotImprovementTwentyFive
nats, exceeding the tolerance by less than a tenth of it, with no uncertainty attached to that comparison by the rule; at
the chosen point it improved by \PilotImprovementFifty. At that budget the cap allowed \PilotCap models per arm, below the
minimum, so the pre-registered outcome is \textsc{not resolvable (compute)}. This is the result of the pre-registered study.
No effect-bearing evaluation of the pilot models was run. A further, post-pilot budget increase was prepared, labelled as
such, and not adopted; neural effort was redirected to the unknown-noise family, whose map leaves more room
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). A pilot that ends by its own rules is evidence about what the design can resolve.
